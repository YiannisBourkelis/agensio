## 1. How comparable projects defined and reached their first stable release, what they promised, what they regretted

### Takeaway
No comparable project reached "stable" on features alone: each either had years of production traffic behind the label (HAProxy, nginx, Pingora inside Cloudflare, WireGuard, Envoy at its origin company), or ran a named beta/RC programme of roughly seven months to sixteen months with users asked to deploy to production (Caddy 2: Sept 2019 to May 2020; Traefik 3: Dec 2022 to Apr 2024; Valkey 8: RC1 Aug 2024, GA Sept 2024 on a codebase with 15 years of history). The most common regret is a 1.0 whose configuration or architecture then had to be broken (Caddy 1.0 was superseded by an incompatible 2.0 twelve months later), and the most durable models (HAProxy, nginx, Envoy) define "stable" as a *branch with a written maintenance window*, not a moment.

### Cited Findings

**Caddy (0.x → 1.0 → 2.0)**
- Caddy 1.0 was announced 24 April 2019 (pre-2024; historical fact) together with Caddy 2 and Caddy Enterprise; the announcement itself made no written stability or semver promise and was mostly about licensing (commercial licences replaced by subscriptions, "everything is the same but without the annoying EULA and separate binary licensing"). Prototypes of Caddy 2 were promised for that same summer, i.e. the successor was announced on the day of 1.0. — [Caddy forum announcement](https://caddy.community/t/announcing-caddy-1-0-caddy-2-and-caddy-enterprise/5569)
- Caddy 2 betas started around 13 September 2019 (beta 1), beta 2 about a week later, beta 3 around 1 October 2019; a forum PSA of late September 2019 warned users not to auto-update to "latest" ahead of 2.0 (pre-2024). — [Caddy announcements index](https://caddy.community/c/announcements/8?page=2) (dates from the search summary of this index)
- At least 18 betas preceded the RCs; the beta-18 post declared "Caddy 2 is now feature-complete. We may still add features before v2, but nothing that is blocking the release" (pre-2024). — [Caddy 2 beta 18](https://caddy.community/t/caddy-2-beta-18-now-available/7256)
- Caddy 2 RC1 is dated 3 April 2020; the project's definition: "Release candidates are production-ready but it means we are reserving another cycle for last-minute bug fixes", and users were asked to "test it out, then deploy it into production, and let us know how it goes" (pre-2024). — [Caddy 2 release candidate 1](https://caddy.community/t/caddy-2-release-candidate-1/7415)
- Caddy 2.0.0 was released 4 May (2020) after at least three RCs; the release note's definition of stable: "\"2.0\" does not mean the software is perfect, only that it is stable", "Its readiness is guided by your feedback!", and "2.1 is already slated to have 30+ improvements" (pre-2024; the wording is still the project's public definition). — [Caddy v2.0.0 release](https://github.com/caddyserver/caddy/releases/tag/v2.0.0)
- Caddy 2 "is not backwards-compatible with Caddy 1"; an upgrade guide was published with the release (pre-2024; still the live upgrade page). — [Caddy v2 upgrade guide](https://caddyserver.com/docs/v2-upgrade)
- Regret, in the maintainer's words: fixing certain Caddy 1 bugs "would be too involved to go back and fix" and would "mostly involve a rewrite, which is… what v2 is"; Caddy 1 was wound down on a fast schedule with an upper bound on its lifetime, which users pushed back on in a multi-page thread (pre-2024). — [Caddy version 1 end of life date thread](https://caddy.community/t/caddy-version-1-end-of-life-date/7835)
- The maintainer later wrote that Caddy 2 went "from design drafts to prototypes to betas to final release" in about 12 months (pre-2024). — [Caddy ownership post](https://caddy.community/t/caddy-and-certmagic-have-new-ownership-no-changes-to-licensing-or-development/9754)
- No published independent audit of Caddy core was found; Trail of Bits' 2023 work covered the third-party `caddy-security` plugin (10 vulnerabilities) (pre-2024), and Caddy core still receives path-handling CVEs in 2026 (CVE-2026-27585: file-matcher backslash/glob sanitisation, fixed in 2.11.1). — [Trail of Bits blog](https://blog.trailofbits.com/2023/09/18/security-flaws-in-an-sso-plugin-for-caddy/); [CVE-2026-27585 advisory mirror](https://corgea.com/advisories/vulnerabilities/CVE-2026-27585)

**HAProxy (the branch/LTS model)**
- Even-numbered branches since 1.9 are LTS: "Such versions will be long-term supported and may be packaged by operating system vendors". Odd-numbered branches "will receive updates for 12 to 18 months only" and "MUST ABSOLUTELY NOT BE PACKAGED BY OPERATING SYSTEM VENDORS". — [HAProxy BRANCHES file](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/haproxy/haproxy/master/BRANCHES)
- A point-zero LTS release has already had about six months of stabilisation (the preceding odd release), so "it is much safer to use even just after it is released". — [HAProxy BRANCHES file](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/haproxy/haproxy/master/BRANCHES)
- Fixes land on the development branch first and are then applied "to older branches, jumping from one to the other in descending order"; users are told "STICK TO THE BRANCH THAT SUITS YOUR NEEDS AND APPLY ALL UPDATES" and warned that "A VERSION WHICH ONLY CONTAINS A SELECTION OF FIXES IS WAY MORE DANGEROUS". After a few years LTS branches "enter a \"critical fixes only\" status"; after EOL "it will not receive any fix at all". — [HAProxy BRANCHES file](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/haproxy/haproxy/master/BRANCHES)
- Third-party trackers and Ubuntu's SRU documentation put LTS support at roughly five years, LTS releases appearing around May/June; HAProxy 3.4.0 (LTS) was released 3 June 2026, 3.3.0 (non-LTS) in November 2025. — [endoflife.date HAProxy](https://endoflife.date/haproxy); [Ubuntu SRU exception for HAProxy](https://documentation.ubuntu.com/project/SRU/reference/exception-HAProxy-Updates); [haproxy.org](https://haproxy.org/)

**nginx (mainline/stable), freenginx, Angie**
- nginx 1.0.0 was released 12 April 2011 (pre-2024; historical). — [HeroDevs nginx version table](https://www.herodevs.com/blog-posts/nginx-versions-eol-dates-and-latest-releases-october-2026) (third-party table)
- nginx's own definition (2019 blog, pre-2024; the model is unchanged in 2026 per third-party trackers listing 1.30 stable / 1.31 mainline): the even-numbered stable branch is forked from mainline yearly and "is updated only when critical issues or security vulnerabilities need to be fixed"; "stable does not mean more reliable or more bug-free. In fact, the mainline is generally regarded as more reliable because we port all bug fixes to it"; "We recommend that in general you deploy the NGINX mainline branch at all times"; stable's promise is aimed at third-party modules ("Changes in the stable branch are very unlikely to affect third-party modules"). — [nginx 1.16/1.17 release blog](https://www.nginx.com/blog/nginx-1-16-1-17-released); [HeroDevs table](https://www.herodevs.com/blog-posts/nginx-versions-eol-dates-and-latest-releases-october-2026)
- freenginx was forked in February 2024 by a core nginx developer after F5 assigned CVEs (CVE-2024-24989 among them) and issued a security release for bugs in the *experimental* HTTP/3 code, against his objection; he wrote that he was no longer able to control which changes are made in nginx and wanted development "free from arbitrary corporate actions". He declined to join Angie because "Angie shares the same problem as Nginx run by F5: it's run by a for-profit corporate entity". First release freenginx-1.25.4 on 20 February 2024. — [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2024/02/16/freenginx_fork/); [The New Stack](https://thenewstack.io/freenginx-a-fork-of-nginx/)

**Envoy**
- Major releases on a fixed quarterly schedule ("the 15th day of each quarter", acceptable delay 2 weeks, hard deadline 3 weeks); stable = "any version released in the last 12 months" (four supported minors at a time); only security and reliability fixes are backported (including "anything that can cause a crash, including crashes triggered by a trusted control plane"); "Security releases are published on a 3-monthly cycle, around the mid point between major releases"; a rotating Release Manager and Fix Lead are named per quarter. 1.40.0 shipped on schedule on 13 October 2026 with EOL 13 October 2027. — [Envoy RELEASES.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/envoyproxy/envoy/main/RELEASES.md)
- Envoy was the first CNCF project to have a public third-party audit: Cure53, February 2018 (pre-2024), eight findings (four vulnerabilities, four weaknesses, none critical), the weak spot being the admin interface, after which the project added explicit documentation about locking it down. — [eWeek on the Envoy audit](https://www.eweek.com/search-engines/envoy-cncf-project-completes-security-audit-delivers-new-release/); [Cure53 report](https://cure53.de/pentest-report_envoy.pdf)

### Inferences
- The label "1.0" binds the configuration format and CLI more than the code. Caddy's history is the cautionary case: a 1.0 with no written compatibility promise, superseded by an incompatible 2.0 a year later, with a contested short EOL for v1. A project that still changes configuration keys without compatibility (as this one does by policy during alpha) should treat "config freeze" as the entry criterion for *beta*, not for 1.0.
- Three working definitions of "stable" exist and a project should pick one in writing: (a) HAProxy/nginx: a branch that receives only fixes, with the development line continuing beside it; (b) Envoy: every release is stable for a fixed window (12 months) with a stated backport class; (c) Caddy: "stable, not perfect", semver by convention. For a solo maintainer, (a) with *one* maintained stable branch plus mainline is the cheapest promise that packagers and distributions understand.
- HAProxy's "x.y.0 of an LTS has already had six months in an odd release" and nginx's "stable is forked from a year of mainline" both encode the same rule: the first stable is a snapshot of something that has already run in public for months without the feature set moving.
- The freenginx split is a warning specific to this project's shape: shipping a protocol implementation as "experimental" inside a stable release does not exempt it from CVEs in the eyes of users, distributors or CNAs. If HTTP/3 (own QUIC) is in 1.0, it is in the security-support scope; if it is not ready for that, it must be off by default and documented as outside the stable promise *before* 1.0, in the security policy.
- Pingora, River and WireGuard show that staying 0.x for years while in real production is respectable; nobody was criticised for labelling late, several were criticised for labelling early (Caddy 1, and the Ferron 1→2 rewrite follows the same pattern).

## 2. Security assurance expected of a network-facing server in a memory-unsafe language in 2026

### Takeaway
By late 2026 the expected baseline for network-facing C++ is: published hardened build flags (OpenSSF guide of 20 August 2026: `_FORTIFY_SOURCE=3`, hardened libc++/libstdc++ assertions, CET/BTI, full RELRO, auto-var-init), continuous coverage-guided fuzzing with sanitizers, at least one independent audit with a public report (typical OSTIF-managed engagement: about 25 person-days and on the order of USD 70k), and a written answer to the government memory-safety question, because CISA/NSA guidance names network-facing C/C++ as the priority case. A new own-implementation HTTP/3 stack is exactly where 2026 disclosures concentrated (the QPACK "header accordion" hit 7 of 10 tested stacks in October 2026).

### Cited Findings

**Independent audits: who, how long, how much**
- X41 D-Sec audited nghttp3 v1.7.0 and ngtcp2 v1.10.0 in February–March 2025, managed by OSTIF and funded by the Sovereign Tech Agency: 25 person-days, manual review of eight QUIC components plus the key agreement/update code, CodeQL and semgrep, the IETF Standards Track documents as the reference threat model; three informational findings (all in ngtcp2, all fixed, none a vulnerability) and AFL++ harnesses for four functions as deliverables. — [X41 D-Sec announcement](https://x41-dsec.de/security/research/job/news/2025/05/22/nghttp3-ngtcp2); [OSTIF summary](https://ostif.org/nghttp3-ngtcp2-audits-complete/); [public report PDF](https://www.x41-dsec.de/static/reports/X41-OSTIF-NG-2025-Audit-Report-Public.pdf)
- An audited library still gets CVEs afterwards: ngtcp2 CVE-2026-40170 was fixed in 1.22.1, newer than the audited code. — search summary citing [CVE listing for ngtcp2](https://www.cvelogic.com/product/nghttp2/ngtcp2)
- curl's 2022 Trail of Bits audit (pre-2024; the reports are still the published reference): arranged by OSTIF with OpenSSF funding, budgeted at 380 person-hours and extended by Trail of Bits by about a week; Trail of Bits lists it as 9.5 engineer-weeks, 14 issues (2 high, 5 low, 5 informational, 2 undetermined), two CVEs; delivered as a threat model and a code review with testing analysis. — [The 2022 curl security audit](https://daniel.haxx.se/blog/2022/12/21/); [Trail of Bits library entry](https://trailofbits.com/library/curl/); [OSTIF: curl audit complete](https://ostif.org/curl-audit-complete/)
- curl's HTTP/3 audit (Trail of Bits, published 23 February 2024, funded by the Sovereign Tech Fund through OSTIF, 4 engineer-weeks): 2 informational issues; it recommended separate audits of ngtcp2, nghttp3, quiche and msh3. The maintainer notes that important findings "have decreased for every new audit". — [curl HTTP/3 security audit](https://daniel.haxx.se/blog/2024/02/23/); [Trail of Bits library entry](https://trailofbits.com/library/curl-http3/)
- Cost: OSTIF publications give an average of about USD 74,000 per managed engagement (a USD 740,000 budget across AWS- and Eclipse-funded audits), one example where an unscoped quote of USD 143,000 became USD 71,000 after OSTIF scoping, and an average of USD 3,000–3,700 per fixed finding or tool delivered. These figures come from a search summary of OSTIF's reports; the individual documents were not opened. — [AWS/OSTIF impact report](https://ostif.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Amazon-Web-Services-AWS-Security-Audit-Impact-Report-.pdf); [2024 OSTIF annual report](https://ostif.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/2024-OSTIF-Annual-Report.pdf)
- OSTIF in 2025 published 24 audits for almost 50 projects with 10 funding bodies. — [OSTIF 2025 annual report](https://ostif.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/OSTIF-2025-Annual-Report.pdf)
- Auditors seen in published 2025–2026 OSTIF work: X41 D-Sec, Trail of Bits, Ada Logics, 7ASecurity, Quarkslab, Shielder; ISC's Kea and Stork audits of 2026 (Ada Logics and 7ASecurity; seven vulnerabilities fixed and re-verified) show the "audit, fix, verify, publish" sequence. — [ISC: two code audits](https://www.isc.org/blogs/2026-two-code-audits/); [OSTIF Sovereign Tech Agency report](https://ostif.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/OSTIF-STA-Report.pdf)
- CNCF requires a completed, published independent third-party audit before a project graduates (as summarised in search results on CNCF's audit programme; pre-2024 policy, Envoy 2018 being the first). — [CNCF blog on independent audits](https://www.cncf.io/blog/2022/08/08/improving-cncf-security-posture-with-independent-security-audits/)
- AI-driven auditing is now part of the picture: curl lists an Aisle engagement (Sept 2025–2026: "hundreds of bugfixes", 35 published vulnerabilities) and a Trail of Bits 2026 audit that used "AI tools from OpenAI". — [curl security audits page](https://curl.se/docs/audits.html)

**What 2026 attackers actually found in HTTP/3 stacks**
- FoxIO's "Header Accordion" (published 20 October 2026): one HEADERS frame with as many distinct names as possible (about 160,000 fields decoded from 1 MB), or 1-byte QPACK dynamic-table references to a ~4 KiB entry (measured 4064:1 amplification). Affected 7 of 10 tested implementations: quic-go (CVE-2025-64702), Cloudflare quiche (CVE-2026-12523), lsquic/OpenLiteSpeed (fixed in lsquic 4.9.0 / OLS 1.9.2), Bun, aioquic/mitmproxy, hyperium/h3, Wireshark (CVE-2025-13945). Not affected: Google quiche, IIS http.sys, ngtcp2/nghttp3 (though nghttp3's limit is signalling-only and the application must enforce it). Stated lesson: a limit on the compressed size is not enough; keep a running total of *decoded* size including RFC 9114's 32-byte per-field overhead, enforce every limit advertised, never default to unlimited, never allocate from a declared length before the data arrives. — [Translated FoxIO write-up](https://lilting.ch/en/articles/http3-qpack-header-amplification); [The Hacker News coverage](https://thehackernews.com/2026/10/new-qpack-flaws-can-crash-http3-servers.html) (page returned 403 to the fetcher; title only)
- Pingora 0.8.1 (June 2026) was a security release bounding default HTTP/2 server limits against memory exhaustion: even a Rust, production-proven stack shipped resource-exhaustion defaults that needed tightening. — [Pingora 0.8.1 notes mirror](https://newreleases.io/project/github/cloudflare/pingora/release/0.8.1)

**Continuous fuzzing**
- OSS-Fuzz acceptance: "must have a significant user base and/or be critical to the global IT infrastructure"; apply by pull request adding `projects/<name>/project.yaml` with homepage, main repository, language and a primary contact e-mail that "belongs to an established project committer (according to VCS logs)" and is linked to a Google account. The original 2016 announcement called the criterion a heuristic "intentionally" left open; the FAQ says requests are judged case by case; Google's reward rules mention a criticality score of ≥ 0.7 as one signal (secondary; not in the docs). — [OSS-Fuzz: accepting new projects](https://google.github.io/oss-fuzz/getting-started/accepting-new-projects/); [OSS-Fuzz FAQ](https://google.github.io/oss-fuzz/faq/); [OSS-Fuzz reward programme rules](https://bughunters.google.com/about/rules/5097259337383936)
- OpenSSF Scorecard's Fuzzing check is satisfied by OSS-Fuzz enrolment *or* ClusterFuzzLite (or a few language-native fuzzers, none for C++); "other approaches may go undetected". — [Scorecard checks](https://github.com/ossf/scorecard/blob/main/docs/checks.md)
- Differential fuzzing of HTTP/1.1: the HTTP Garden (Dartmouth / Narf Industries, May 2024) runs one request stream through many origin servers and gateways and reports meaningful, durable discrepancies; over 100 parsing bugs found in popular servers, 68 fixed at publication; bug classes include NUL, bare CR or LF permitted in header values; its premise is that "the most significant parsing anomalies occur within origin servers". — [The HTTP Garden (arXiv 2405.17737)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17737)
- SQLite's fuzzing practice as the high-water mark: OSS-Fuzz since 2016, a structure-aware fuzzer of its own (dbsqlfuzz, 336 seeds, ~1 billion mutations/day), and a "fuzzcheck" corpus of a few thousand historically interesting inputs rerun on every `make test`. — [How SQLite Is Tested](https://sqlite.org/testing.html)

**Hardened builds: the 2026 flag set**
- OpenSSF Compiler Options Hardening Guide for C and C++ (version dated 2026-08-20) recommends for all builds: `-O2 -Wall -Wformat -Wformat=2 -Wconversion -Wimplicit-fallthrough -Werror=format-security -U_FORTIFY_SOURCE -D_FORTIFY_SOURCE=3 -D_GLIBCXX_ASSERTIONS -D_LIBCPP_HARDENING_MODE=_LIBCPP_HARDENING_MODE_FAST -fstrict-flex-arrays=3 -fstack-clash-protection -fstack-protector-strong -Wl,-z,nodlopen -Wl,-z,noexecstack -Wl,-z,relro -Wl,-z,now -Wl,--as-needed -Wl,--no-copy-dt-needed-entries`; executables `-fPIE -pie`; x86_64 `-fcf-protection=full -fzero-call-used-regs=used-gpr`; AArch64 `-mbranch-protection=standard`; production code `-fno-delete-null-pointer-checks -fno-strict-overflow -fno-strict-aliasing -ftrivial-auto-var-init=zero`. — [OpenSSF hardening guide](https://best.openssf.org/Compiler-Hardening-Guides/Compiler-Options-Hardening-Guide-for-C-and-C++)
- Costs it states: `_FORTIFY_SOURCE` 1/2 about 0.1 % (level 3 "can affect performance in rare cases", and is incompatible with AddressSanitizer builds); `_GLIBCXX_ASSERTIONS` up to 6 % reported in some versions, no ABI effect; libc++ fast mode = "security-critical, constant-time checks"; `-fcf-protection` mild; shadow stack needs Linux 6.6+, glibc 2.39+ and is enabled at run time through `GLIBC_TUNABLES`; `-fzero-call-used-regs=used-gpr` under 1 % on most workloads; GCC 14's `-fhardened` enables a predefined set. — [OpenSSF hardening guide](https://best.openssf.org/Compiler-Hardening-Guides/Compiler-Options-Hardening-Guide-for-C-and-C++)
- C++26 includes standard-library hardening (P3471, approved at Hagenberg, February 2025) built on contracts (P2900); core-language safety *profiles* (P3081) were voted out of C++26 and are targeted at C++29; "Safe C++" did not advance. Vendors were still completing the feature in August 2026. — [P3471R4 Standard library hardening](https://open-std.org/JTC1/SC22/WG21/docs/papers/2025/p3471r4.html); [C++ safety state of the union, May 2026](https://wrocpp.github.io/posts/cpp-safety-state-of-the-union-may-2026/); [C++26 memory safety: what actually ships](https://www.wholetomato.com/blog/c26-memory-safety-what-actually-ships-for-existing-code/)

**Government and industry guidance on memory safety**
- CISA/FBI "Product Security Bad Practices" (2024–2025): developing new product lines for critical infrastructure in a memory-unsafe language where alternatives exist is a bad practice; for existing products, manufacturers were asked to publish a memory-safety roadmap by 1 January 2026, prioritising "network-facing code or code handling sensitive functions like cryptography" (products with support ending before 2030 exempt). Search results held only 2024-era coverage; whether the date was enforced or revised was not established. — [The New Stack summary](https://thenewstack.io/feds-critical-software-must-drop-c-c-by-2026-or-face-risk/)
- NSA/CISA joint sheet "Memory Safe Languages: Reducing Vulnerabilities in Modern Software Development" (24 June 2025): memory-safe languages for new development, prioritised rewrites of high-risk components (network services, cryptographic functions), modular APIs between old and new, and, where a transition is not feasible, alternatives "such as memory tagging hardware or compiler hardening" (the last phrase from secondary coverage). — [CISA resource page](https://www.cisa.gov/resources-tools/resources/memory-safe-languages-reducing-vulnerabilities-modern-software-development); [Infosecurity Magazine](https://www.infosecurity-magazine.com/news/nsa-cisa-urge-memory-safe-languages/)

**Static analysis, per the criteria that grade it**
- OpenSSF Best Practices badge (passing): static analysis "MUST be applied to any proposed major production release ... before its release"; dynamic analysis is only SUGGESTED, with a fuzzer plus memory-safety detection suggested for memory-unsafe languages; compiler warnings or a linter MUST be enabled. — [Best Practices criteria (passing)](https://www.bestpractices.dev/en/criteria/0)
- Scorecard's SAST check looks for CodeQL or SonarCloud running on recent PRs/workflows. — [Scorecard checks](https://github.com/ossf/scorecard/blob/main/docs/checks.md)
- Counterpoint from SQLite: static analysis has been "unhelpful for finding bugs" there, behind coverage-driven testing and fuzzing. — [How SQLite Is Tested](https://sqlite.org/testing.html)

**Sandboxing and privilege separation**
- OpenBSD's model (httpd, relayd and most base daemons): start as root, split into processes in separate security domains talking over pipes/imsg, chroot to an empty directory, drop privileges, then `pledge`/`unveil` per process; a 2021 review by the project's lead found implementation errors in about 10 % of privsep programs (a child doing more than the design allowed). — [Privilege drop, privilege separation and restricted-service mode in OpenBSD](https://sha256.net/privsep.html); [OpenBSD pledge paper, dotSecurity 2016](https://OpenBSD.org/papers/dot2016.pdf) (pre-2024; the design still holds); [2026 post on relayd and httpd](https://rsadowski.de/posts/2026/dead-software-walking-relayd-and-httpd/) (says development of both had stalled and is being picked up again)
- `systemd-analyze security` gives an exposure score from 0 (tight) to 10; a stock `nginx.service` is reported at 9.6 UNSAFE, most default units at 8–9; guides suggest MEDIUM (4–6) or better as a target and show a unit reaching about 2 with `ProtectSystem=strict` plus explicit writable paths, `CapabilityBoundingSet=`, `SystemCallFilter=@system-service` and the `Protect*`/`Restrict*` family. These are tutorial figures, not a standard. — [ctrl.blog: systemd service sandboxing 101](https://www.ctrl.blog/entry/systemd-service-hardening/); [Arch wiki: systemd sandboxing](https://wiki.archlinux.org/title/Systemd/Sandboxing); [DEV guide with example scores](https://dev.to/lyraalishaikh/harden-linux-services-with-systemd-analyze-security-from-score-to-enforceable-policy-5415)

**Supply chain**
- Scorecard: Signed-Releases scores 8/10 for signature files on releases (`.asc`, `.sig`, `.minisig`, `.sigstore`) and 10/10 only with SLSA provenance (`.intoto.jsonl`) on every release; SBOM check wants an SBOM as a release artifact; Pinned-Dependencies wants hashes in workflows and Dockerfiles; Token-Permissions wants read-only default workflow tokens; Dangerous-Workflow and Webhooks are "critical". — [Scorecard checks](https://github.com/ossf/scorecard/blob/main/docs/checks.md)
- Scorecard structurally penalises a solo project: Code-Review (high risk) wants human-approved changes ("bot reviews don't count"), and the docs concede this is infeasible "for projects with one active participant"; Contributors wants recent commits from at least three organisations; Maintained wants at least one commit a week over 90 days. — [Scorecard checks](https://github.com/ossf/scorecard/blob/main/docs/checks.md)
- OpenSSF OSPS Baseline, current version v2026.08.28: MUST-only controls organised by maturity level; "only the current version should be used for new compliance efforts". — [OSPS Baseline](https://baseline.openssf.org/)
- Best Practices badge (passing) also requires: HTTPS for site/repo/downloads, unique version per release, human-readable release notes per release, a delivery mechanism that counters MITM, default crypto limited to published and reviewed algorithms, and "at least one primary developer who knows how to design secure software". — [Best Practices criteria (passing)](https://www.bestpractices.dev/en/criteria/0)

### Inferences
- An OSS-Fuzz application from a project with "a handful of users" will most likely be declined or left pending; the route that scores the same on Scorecard and needs no acceptance is ClusterFuzzLite in the project's own CI, with OSS-Fuzz applied for once there are packagers or a distribution carrying it (distribution inclusion is the usual evidence of "significant user base").
- The audits of ngtcp2/nghttp3 (25 person-days for two mature libraries) and curl's HTTP/3 layer (4 engineer-weeks) bound the scope question: an own QUIC + QPACK + HTTP/2 + HTTP/1 parser + TLS-record layer + a root helper is several times that surface. A staged audit is the realistic shape: first the pre-authentication network surface (parsers, QUIC packet handling, HPACK/QPACK, TLS stream) and the privileged helper; later the control plane. At OSTIF's published averages that is one to two engagements of about USD 70k each, normally paid by a funder (Sovereign Tech Agency, OpenSSF/Alpha-Omega, NLnet-style grants), not by the maintainer.
- The header-accordion disclosure is the most concrete 2026 checklist item for an own HTTP/3 stack: a decoded-size running total with the 32-byte per-field overhead, enforced during decoding, for both HPACK and QPACK, with a test row in the attack suite and a fuzz invariant ("retained bytes ≤ k × bytes received").
- Against the OpenSSF guide of August 2026, a build that sets `_FORTIFY_SOURCE=2` is one notch behind the recommended `=3`; `-fstack-clash-protection`, `-fcf-protection=full`, `-fstrict-flex-arrays=3`, `-Wl,-z,now`, `-fzero-call-used-regs=used-gpr` and the libc++/libstdc++ assertion macros are the items a reviewer will look for in the build log. Each has a stated cost, so each can go through the project's A/B gate and the result be published; "measured, adopted" or "measured, declined because X %" are both credible answers.
- "Why C++ in 2026?" needs a written answer in the security documentation, because CISA's guidance names network-facing code first. The credible form, by the guidance's own alternatives, is: hardened standard library, the hardening flags above, sanitizer and fuzz coverage figures, sandboxing/privilege separation, and an audit; not an argument that the guidance is wrong.
- Scorecard cannot be maxed by a solo project (Code-Review, Contributors); publishing the score with a short note on the two structural zeros is more credible than chasing them. The Best Practices *passing* badge is fully attainable solo and is the cheaper signal.

### Gaps
- No published audit was found for HAProxy, Traefik core, Cloudflare quiche or s2n-quic in this research (searches returned nothing); absence of results is not proof of absence. Trail of Bits' 2024 report explicitly recommended a quiche audit, which suggests none was public then.
- Day rates for Cure53, Trail of Bits, NCC, X41 or Radically Open Security were not found; only OSTIF's averages.
- The parts of the OpenSSF guide on `-fsanitize=cfi`, `-fhardened` details and sanitizers in production lie beyond the portion of the page that was read (the page is 187k characters; the first 100k were read).
- No data was found on adoption of Landlock or seccomp filters *inside* web servers (as opposed to via systemd units) in 2025–2026.
- Coverity Scan, Clang Static Analyzer and GCC `-fanalyzer` practice at comparable projects, coverage targets for fuzzing (e.g. a percentage a 1.0 should show) and corpus-management conventions: not researched to a citable source.
- Reproducible-builds status of comparable servers and concrete SLSA levels they claim: not researched.
- The current enforcement status of CISA's 1 January 2026 roadmap date is unknown.

## 3. Vulnerability handling, CVE assignment, and the EU Cyber Resilience Act

### Takeaway
A 1.0 needs a SECURITY.md with a private channel (GitHub private vulnerability reporting plus an e-mail address is curl's 2026 choice), a 14-day response commitment, a supported-versions table, and a CVE route (GitHub is a CNA for hosted projects; curl became its own CNA to keep bogus CVEs out). Under the CRA, a solo, non-monetised MIT project is outside scope: it is not a "manufacturer" unless the software is supplied in the course of a commercial activity, and it cannot be a "steward", which the regulation defines as a legal person; the Commission's guidance of July 2026 confirms voluntary donations and sponsorship do not by themselves make a project commercial. Selling the server, paid editions, or support priced above cost changes that.

### Cited Findings

**Disclosure policy and channels**
- Best Practices (passing): the project must publish a vulnerability-report process, and initial response time to any vulnerability report received in the last six months "MUST be less than or equal to 14 days"; no medium-or-higher vulnerability publicly known for more than 60 days may remain unpatched. — [Best Practices criteria (passing)](https://www.bestpractices.dev/en/criteria/0)
- Scorecard's Security-Policy check wants a SECURITY.md with contact details, the reporting process and disclosure timelines. — [Scorecard checks](https://github.com/ossf/scorecard/blob/main/docs/checks.md)
- curl, after closing its bounty (January 2026), takes reports through GitHub's private vulnerability reporting or by e-mail to its security address; HackerOne is no longer the recommended channel. — [The end of the curl bug bounty](https://daniel.haxx.se/blog/2026/01/26/the-end-of-the-curl-bug-bounty/)
- Envoy's process is a worked example for a project with distributors: named Release Manager and Fix Lead per quarter, security releases on a three-monthly cycle, emergency releases for zero-days "with little or no warning", and a defined backport class (security, crashes, selected bugfixes) for every version under 12 months old. — [Envoy RELEASES.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/envoyproxy/envoy/main/RELEASES.md)
- HAProxy's rule for users is part of its policy: apply all updates of your branch; never run a hand-picked selection of fixes. — [HAProxy BRANCHES file](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/haproxy/haproxy/master/BRANCHES)

**CVE assignment**
- "GitHub acts as a CNA for any open source project that's hosted on GitHub": the maintainer drafts a repository security advisory and clicks "Request CVE"; review is usually within 72 hours; requesting does not publish the advisory; GitHub cannot assign if the project is covered by another CNA. — [GitHub docs: publishing a repository security advisory](https://docs.github.com/en/code-security/how-tos/report-and-fix-vulnerabilities/fix-reported-vulnerabilities/publishing-a-repository-security-advisory); [GitHub blog: removing the stigma of a CVE](https://github.blog/security/vulnerability-research/removing-the-stigma-of-a-cve) (pre-2024; the mechanism still holds)
- Friction in 2026: an oss-security thread of June 2026 reports OpenWrt waiting about a week for CVE IDs through GitHub; a Socket analysis notes many projects now publish GHSA-only and request CVEs later or never. — [oss-security, 10 June 2026](https://openwall.com/lists/oss-security/2026/06/10/9); [Socket blog on GHSA vs CVE gaps](https://socket.dev/blog/openclaw-advisory-surge-highlights-gaps-between-ghsa-and-cve-tracking)
- curl has been its own CNA since 16 January 2024 "to ideally help us filter out bad CVE input better". — [Daniel's week, 19 January 2024](https://lists.haxx.se/pipermail/daniel/2024-January/000050.html)
- The freenginx case shows what goes wrong without an agreed rule: a CVE and a security release for experimental HTTP/3 code, issued over a core developer's objection, split the project. — [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2024/02/16/freenginx_fork/)

**EU Cyber Resilience Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/2847)**
- Dates: reporting obligations (Article 14) apply from 11 September 2026; the regulation applies in full from 11 December 2027. — [OpenSSF CRA stewards one-pager](https://policy.openssf.org/CRA/stewards-one-pager.html); [Open Source For You on the July 2026 guidance](https://www.opensourceforu.com/2026/07/eu-clarifies-cra-rules-for-non-commercial-open-source/)
- Scope: the CRA applies to software only if "supplied for distribution or use in the course of a commercial activity"; "The CRA does not apply to any contributors to someone else's OSS project" (recital 18); a developer who publishes code in their own repository without trying to monetise it does "not have to worry about the CRA at all". Listed as not commercial in themselves: financial support from manufacturers without profit, regular releases, hosting on an open repository, accepting donations without intent to profit, support from a not-for-profit. Listed as commercial (recital 15): charging for the product, charging for technical support beyond actual costs, intent to monetise (e.g. a platform monetising other services), requiring personal data beyond security/compatibility needs, accepting donations exceeding costs. — [OpenSSF CRA Brief Guide for OSS Developers](https://best.openssf.org/CRA-Brief-Guide-for-OSS-Developers)
- Steward definition (Article 3(14)): "a legal person, other than a manufacturer" that has the purpose of "systematically providing support on a sustained basis for the development of specific products with digital elements, qualifying as free and open-source software and intended for commercial activities" and "ensures the viability of those products". Steward duties (Article 24): a documented, verifiable cybersecurity policy covering vulnerability handling and secure development; cooperation with market-surveillance authorities on request; Article 14 reporting of actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents to the extent the steward is involved in development or runs the affected infrastructure. — [OpenSSF CRA stewards one-pager](https://policy.openssf.org/CRA/stewards-one-pager.html)
- Stewards are exempt from the administrative fines (Article 64(10)) and have no conformity assessment or CE marking duties (third-party reading of the text). — search summary citing [independent CRA guide](https://cyberresilienceact.eu/faq.html) and [sota.io Article 24 guide](https://sota.io/blog/cra-article-8-open-source-software-steward-obligations-foundation-nonprofit-developer-guide-2026)
- The Linux Foundation playbook: "Not every upstream project needs or wants a Steward. A Steward typically will have access to more resources than an unaffiliated single-maintainer project". The OpenSSF brief guide: no project is required to have a steward. — [LF CRA Stewards Playbook](https://policy.openssf.org/CRA/stewards-playbook.html); [OpenSSF CRA Brief Guide](https://best.openssf.org/CRA-Brief-Guide-for-OSS-Developers)
- Commission guidance: a draft in March 2026 and a final version in July 2026 (reported as communication C(2026) 5252 with an annex; about 80 pages; not legally binding). As reported: freely available open-source software is generally outside the CRA unless marketed as part of a commercial activity; voluntary donations, sponsorships, public funding and separately provided consulting, training or support "do not automatically" make a project commercial as long as the software stays freely available; what does: selling the software, paid enterprise editions, monetising services through the software, requiring personal data beyond security/interoperability, "making donations effectively mandatory for software access or essential updates"; patch contributors bear no CRA responsibility, obligations fall on "individuals or organisations controlling releases, project roadmaps and governance" *where the activity is commercial*. — [Open Source For You](https://www.opensourceforu.com/2026/07/eu-clarifies-cra-rules-for-non-commercial-open-source/); [Lewis Silkin: CRA guidance now out](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/07/31/eu-cyber-resilience-act-guidance-now-out-heres-what-you-need-to-know-102nfex); [Linklaters on the March 2026 draft](https://techinsights.linklaters.com/post/102mmlo/eu-cyber-resilience-act-commission-issues-first-draft-guidance-10-key-points-y)
- Sources conflict on paid support: one law-firm reading has separately provided consulting/support not making the FOSS commercial, another commentary says paid support, SLA-backed hosting or professional services built around the product *are* commercial activity; and recital 15 (donations exceeding costs = commercial) sits beside the guidance's "voluntary donations do not automatically". — search summary contrasting [Lewis Silkin](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/07/31/eu-cyber-resilience-act-guidance-now-out-heres-what-you-need-to-know-102nfex) with [Greenbone's CRA and open source article](https://www.greenbone.net/en/blog/cra-open-source-software/)
- What downstream will ask of an upstream even when the CRA does not bind it: a stated way to report vulnerabilities (SECURITY.md), coordinated disclosure, and possibly "documentation, patches, or other artifacts", including an SBOM, because manufacturers integrating the software must exercise due diligence. — [OpenSSF CRA Brief Guide](https://best.openssf.org/CRA-Brief-Guide-for-OSS-Developers)

### Inferences
- For a solo-maintained, MIT-licensed server given away with no paid edition, the maintainer is neither manufacturer nor steward: not a manufacturer because nothing is supplied in a commercial activity; not a steward because a natural person is not a "legal person" and there is no organisation "ensuring viability". This is a reading of secondary sources and the non-binding guidance, not legal advice; the two edges to watch are (1) support or hosting sold at a profit, or a paid edition, which makes the seller a manufacturer of *that* offering, and (2) donations that exceed costs on a sustained basis.
- The CRA still shapes 1.0 indirectly: any EU company that ships the server inside a product (a hosting panel, an appliance) is a manufacturer and must do due diligence on it. What they will look for is exactly the steward checklist: written vulnerability-handling policy, supported-versions statement with dates, SBOM per release, a security contact that answers. Having these makes the project adoptable by regulated users; lacking them is a reason to pick nginx.
- A written rule for "experimental" features in the security policy (are they in CVE scope? off by default?) should exist before 1.0; the freenginx split was about precisely that ambiguity.
- CVE route for a small project: GitHub advisories with "Request CVE" is sufficient at 1.0; becoming a CNA is something curl did after 25 years and for a reason (bogus CVE filings) that a young project does not yet have.

### Gaps
- The CRA's official text (recitals 15, 18, 19; Articles 3, 14, 24, 64) and the Commission guidance C(2026) 5252 were not read directly; all CRA findings above are from OpenSSF/Linux Foundation summaries, law-firm notes and a news article. The exact wording on donations and paid support should be checked against the Official Journal and the guidance annex before relying on it.
- Whether the Commission's guidance says anything specific about a *natural person* who controls releases of a widely used non-commercial project (beyond "generally outside scope") was not established.
- Embargo practice for a server with distribution packages (linux-distros list, pre-notification of packagers) was not researched.
- Analyses by the Open Source Initiative and the Eclipse Foundation's ORC working group were surfaced in search results (an ORC white paper is mentioned by heise) but not read.

## 4. Conformance and third-party verification a 1.0 should be able to show

### Takeaway
The verifiable set in 2026 is: the public QUIC interop matrix, h2spec and an HTTP/3 error-case suite, differential HTTP/1.1 parsing against other servers (HTTP Garden), a TLS scanner run, fault-injection and long soak runs with numbers, and benchmarks that someone else can rerun. TechEmpower's benchmarks were archived on 24 March 2026, so there is no longer a neutral referee board; HttpArena is the community successor but is not official, and reproducibility (harness checked in, raw output published) carries the credibility.

### Cited Findings
- QUIC interop runner: the public matrix at interop.seemann.io documents "the current interop status of various QUIC client and server implementations that have chosen to participate", is "updated several times per day", and verifies for each test case that the client downloaded the expected files with matching contents; it was introduced in a 2020 workshop paper (pre-2024; the service is live). Participation means providing a container image that speaks the runner's test protocol. — [QUIC interop runner results](https://interop.seemann.io/); [IETF blog: automated QUIC interop testing](https://www.ietf.org/blog/quic-automated-interop-testing/)
- HTTP Garden: differential fuzzing of HTTP/1.1 request streams across origin servers and gateways; more than 100 parsing bugs in popular servers, 68 fixed at publication (May 2024); typical findings are control bytes accepted in header values and other request-smuggling primitives. — [The HTTP Garden (arXiv 2405.17737)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17737)
- 2026's HTTP/3 lesson for conformance suites: enforce advertised limits and bound *decoded* header size (7 of 10 stacks failed). — [FoxIO header accordion write-up (translation)](https://lilting.ch/en/articles/http3-qpack-header-amplification)
- testssl.sh is the open command-line TLS scanner (protocols, ciphers, known flaws; includes an SSL Labs-style rating that its changelog calls "not complete"), actively released. — [testssl.sh releases](https://www.github.com/drwetter/testssl.sh/releases); [testssl.sh changelog](https://github.com/drwetter/testssl.sh/blob/3.1dev/CHANGELOG.md)
- Fault injection and soak as SQLite does it: simulated malloc failure at every allocation point, I/O-error injection through a failing VFS followed by integrity checks, simulated crashes, a pre-release soak of ~248.5 million tests, automatic leak detection on every test run, and a ~200-item manual release checklist. — [How SQLite Is Tested](https://sqlite.org/testing.html)
- TechEmpower Framework Benchmarks: sunset and repository archived (read-only) on 24 March 2026; the post gives no specific reason and names no successor; history and past results remain available. — [TechEmpower FrameworkBenchmarks issue #10932](https://github.com/TechEmpower/FrameworkBenchmarks/issues/10932)
- HttpArena is described by community posts as started to address TechEmpower's pain points (one test environment; HTTP/1.1, HTTP/2, HTTP/3, gRPC, WebSocket) and as "the most visible successor in 2026"; no official statement from TechEmpower names it. — [DEV post on the archive and what's next](https://dev.to/kaliumhexacyanoferrat/techempower-framework-benchmarks-are-now-archived-whats-next-3l0a)
- nginx on what "stable" should not be read as, relevant to how benchmark and reliability claims are worded: "stable does not mean more reliable or more bug-free" (pre-2024; still the model). — [nginx 1.16/1.17 release blog](https://www.nginx.com/blog/nginx-1-16-1-17-released)

### Inferences
- For a server with its own QUIC stack, a row in the *public* interop matrix (not only a local run of the runner) is the single most recognisable third-party signal, because every other serious stack is there and the result is produced by someone else's infrastructure. The choices to exit 127 on some cases (0-RTT, ECN, v2, migration) should be listed in the documentation as unsupported features, not left for readers to find in the matrix.
- Being added to the HTTP Garden's server set (or running it locally and publishing the discrepancy list with each one's disposition) converts "we have 17 smuggling unit tests" into "we parse the same as nginx, Apache and h2o on N million mutated streams, except where listed".
- With TechEmpower gone, benchmark claims are judged on method: one command to reproduce, competitor configs published and tuned, raw tool output, CPU-per-request beside requests-per-second, and a third-party board row (HttpArena) where the harness is not the project's own. Claims of "faster than nginx" on a loopback box with the load generator on the same cores are the ones that get dismissed; the project's own notes already identify that failure mode.
- Soak evidence a 1.0 can show cheaply: N days of continuous mixed load (connection churn, slow clients, h1/h2/h3, reloads every few minutes) with RSS, descriptor count and request-error count plotted, run on every RC. SQLite's standard (fault injection at every allocation and I/O point) is the direction for the parts that touch disk and the control plane.

### Gaps
- h2spec's and h3spec's current maintenance status, REDbot, the cache-tests suite, SSL Labs grading rules and Mozilla Observatory were named in the brief but searches returned nothing citable in the time available; they are known tools, but no 2026 status is recorded here.
- No source was found describing other servers' soak-test durations or chaos practices (Envoy, HAProxy, Caddy) concretely.
- HttpArena's governance, methodology and how results are accepted were not read from its own site.
- TechEmpower's actual reasons for sunsetting are not stated in the primary post; secondary blogs speculate.

## 5. Operational maturity: packaging, upgrades, documentation, cadence, governance, AI-assisted development

### Takeaway
After 1.0 the promise users buy is a maintenance window and an upgrade path: a written branch policy (HAProxy's BRANCHES file and Envoy's RELEASES.md are the two models to copy), a migration guide shipped with every breaking release (Caddy 2 and Traefik 3 both did this on release day), and a statement of who else can ship a fix. For AI-assisted code the 2026 norm is disclosure plus human accountability: 78 % of written project AI policies allow AI contributions and 51 % require disclosure, but the public reaction to AI-written network-facing code is sceptical and is settled by outside review (Cloudflare's vinext had 7 vulnerabilities disclosed by a competitor within about a week).

### Cited Findings

**Release cadence, branches, deprecation**
- HAProxy: one document states the whole life cycle (development, LTS vs short-lived, critical-fixes-only, EOL, who should run what, packagers must not ship odd branches). — [HAProxy BRANCHES file](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/haproxy/haproxy/master/BRANCHES)
- Envoy: a schedule table with expected vs actual dates and the slip in days for every release and security release (e.g. 1.38.0 +9 days; 2026 Q2 security release +22 days), plus EOL dates. — [Envoy RELEASES.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/envoyproxy/envoy/main/RELEASES.md)
- nginx: stable branch exists chiefly so third-party modules do not break; the vendor recommends mainline. — [nginx 1.16/1.17 release blog](https://www.nginx.com/blog/nginx-1-16-1-17-released) (pre-2024; still the model)
- Best Practices (passing) requires a unique version per release and human-readable release notes for each. — [Best Practices criteria (passing)](https://www.bestpractices.dev/en/criteria/0)

**Upgrade and migration**
- Caddy 2 shipped a v1→v2 upgrade guide with the release and told users "Please spend time in the documentation"; v1's short EOL drew a long thread of objections (pre-2024). — [Caddy v2.0.0 release](https://github.com/caddyserver/caddy/releases/tag/v2.0.0); [Caddy v1 EOL thread](https://caddy.community/t/caddy-version-1-end-of-life-date/7835)
- Traefik published "3.0 GA has landed, here's how to migrate" on GA day and keeps a per-version release/deprecation table in the docs. — [Traefik 3.0 GA and migration](https://traefik.io/blog/traefik-3-0-ga-has-landed-heres-how-to-migrate); [Traefik release table](https://doc.traefik.io/traefik/v3.0/deprecation/releases/)
- Pingora, still 0.x, removes public APIs between minors (0.9.0) and keeps "a rolling MSRV policy of 6 months": what a 0.x label legitimately permits. — [Pingora 0.9.0 notes mirror](https://newreleases.io/project/github/cloudflare/pingora/release/0.9.0); [Pingora repository](https://github.com/cloudflare/pingora)

**Governance and bus factor**
- freenginx: the fork's stated purpose was independence from a single corporate owner; its author rejected Angie for the same reason. — [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2024/02/16/freenginx_fork/)
- River: a foundation-backed (ISRG) project that nonetheless shows no release since 2024: institutional backing does not guarantee continuity. — [River repository](https://github.com/memorysafety/river); [River progress post](https://memorysafety.org/blog/river-release)
- OpenBSD httpd/relayd: a 2026 post describes development as having stalled until new contributors picked them up. — [Dead software walking: relayd and httpd](https://rsadowski.de/posts/2026/dead-software-walking-relayd-and-httpd/)
- Scorecard quantifies the solo-maintainer problem: Contributors (three organisations), Code-Review (human approval), Maintained (weekly commits). — [Scorecard checks](https://github.com/ossf/scorecard/blob/main/docs/checks.md)
- Caddy changed ownership in 2020 with the statement "no changes to licensing or development" (pre-2024): a sponsor/acquirer as the continuity answer. — [Caddy and CertMagic have new ownership](https://caddy.community/t/caddy-and-certmagic-have-new-ownership-no-changes-to-licensing-or-development/9754)
- The Linux Foundation notes a steward organisation "typically will have access to more resources than an unaffiliated single-maintainer project". — [LF CRA Stewards Playbook](https://policy.openssf.org/CRA/stewards-playbook.html)

**AI-assisted development and trust (2025–2026)**
- A May 2026 study of GitHub repositories' AI policies: 78 % allow GenAI contributions, 22 % discourage them, 51 % require disclosure of AI-generated contributions; a separate count found only 118 of 1,000 popular repositories had any written AI policy (the latter figure from a search summary). — [AI Policy, Disclosure, and Human in the Loop (arXiv 2605.16706)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.16706)
- RedMonk's tracker (Feb 2026, updated through June 2026) classifies organisations as permissive, ban or undecided, with concerns of quality, copyright and ethics, and lists among others the Linux Foundation, Apache, Eclipse, the Linux kernel, Gentoo, curl, CPython, Zig, Kubernetes, Node.js, Ladybird; it notes adoption of policies has accelerated since 2023. — [RedMonk: generative AI policy landscape in open source](https://redmonk.com/kholterhoff/2026/02/26/generative-ai-policy-landscape-in-open-source/)
- Cloudflare's vinext (February 2026), announced with "Almost every line of code in vinext was written by AI": within about a week Vercel disclosed 2 critical, 2 high, 2 medium and 1 low vulnerabilities; a security firm's write-up (dated 27 February 2026) reported dozens more (45 reported, 24 manually validated: unverified count); commentary split between "speed can outrun the security review process" and "new frameworks will have vulnerabilities". — [OfficeChai summary](https://officechai.com/ai/after-cloudflare-created-vercels-next-js-in-a-week-using-ai-vercel-discloses-7-bugs-in-the-new-framework); [Hacktron: vibe-hacking vinext](https://hacktron.ai/blog/hacking-cloudflare-vinext); [Ory: please don't vibe code your own crypto](https://www.ory.com/blog/cloudflare-please-dont-vibe-code-your-own-crypto)
- An nginx configuration scanner (Gixy) was forked in 2025–2026 by a maintainer who described the AI-written successor as "AI-written code which simply did not do what it advertised" with "obvious regressions and broken code". — [Joshua Rogers: gixy-ng AI slop, gixy-next maintained](https://joshua.hu/gixy-ng-ai-slop-gixy-next-maintained)
- Hacker News reaction to AI-assisted servers (anecdotal threads, 2025–2026): "the combination of AI-written, network-facing, and C makes me shudder"; one AI-assisted C server labels itself "experimental ... developed with AI assistance and is not recommended for production use". — search summary of [Show HN threads](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45653350) and related items
- The other side of AI in 2026: AI-assisted *finding* is credited with real results (curl: 35 published vulnerabilities via Aisle; Trail of Bits using AI tools in a 40-hour audit) while AI-generated *reports* destroyed curl's bounty (confirmed rate from above 15 % to below 5 %). — [curl security audits page](https://curl.se/docs/audits.html); [The end of the curl bug bounty](https://daniel.haxx.se/blog/2026/01/26/the-end-of-the-curl-bug-bounty/)

### Inferences
- The cheapest high-value operational artefacts for 1.0 are two files modelled on HAProxy's BRANCHES and Envoy's RELEASES.md: which branch is stable, how long it gets fixes, what class of fix is backported, when the next stable is cut, and what packagers should ship. Distribution maintainers read exactly this.
- A config-compatibility promise must be explicit at 1.0 (within 1.x: old files keep loading; deprecations warn for at least one stable cycle before removal; `-t` reports deprecated keys). The project's alpha policy of never keeping old config forms is fine until beta and incompatible with a stable label; a migration tool or documented mechanism needs to exist and be exercised at least once (beta→RC) before 1.0.
- Bus factor: none of the comparable projects solved it with a document. The patterns that exist are a company (Caddy, Traefik, HAProxy Technologies, F5), a foundation (Valkey, Envoy/CNCF) or a second committer with release rights. For a solo project the honest minimum is: a named second person (or organisation) holding the signing keys and the security inbox in escrow, a documented release procedure that a stranger can run (reproducible release job), and a statement of what happens to the stable branch if the maintainer stops.
- AI disclosure: given vinext and the HN reaction, a server developed with heavy AI assistance will be assumed unreviewed unless shown otherwise. What shifts the judgement is external and checkable: an independent audit report, public fuzzing with coverage numbers, the interop matrix, differential-testing results, and a plain statement of how AI was used and who reviews and answers for each change. Disclosure without those invites the vinext treatment; those without disclosure invites a later "gotcha". The curl audits page shows the constructive framing: AI as an additional reviewer whose findings are listed publicly.
- Telemetry-free defaults, licence clarity (MIT, vendored-dependency licences listed) and a container image are table stakes that reviewers check rather than praise; no source in this research treats them as differentiators.

### Gaps
- No primary source was found for how a project *built* largely with AI assistance (as opposed to accepting AI contributions) has disclosed it in a way the community accepted; the examples found are of negative reactions. The vinext vulnerability counts beyond Vercel's seven are from a single security-firm blog.
- Packaging expectations (which distributions' official repositories comparable projects were in at 1.0, container image conventions, Homebrew/Nix presence) were not researched to a citable source.
- Documentation completeness benchmarks (what nginx→X or Apache→X migration guides comparable projects ship) were not researched beyond Caddy's and Traefik's upgrade guides.
- Sponsorship/foundation routes for solo network-infrastructure maintainers in 2026 (Sovereign Tech Agency fellowships, NLnet, Alpha-Omega) were not researched beyond their appearance as audit funders.
- Envoy's and Traefik's written deprecation policies were not retrieved.

## 6. Minimum real-world exposure before a stable label; canaries, public test instances, bug bounties

### Takeaway
Comparable projects had months to years of other people's production traffic before "stable": Caddy 2 asked users to deploy RCs to production and took about eight months from beta 1 to GA; Traefik 3 took sixteen; HAProxy treats six months in a development-status release as the precondition for an LTS x.y.0; Pingora, WireGuard and nginx had years. Paid bug bounties are no longer a realistic channel for a young project in 2026: curl ended its bounty in January, HackerOne's Internet Bug Bounty has been paused for new submissions since 27 March, and huntr dropped its general open-source programme.

### Cited Findings
- Caddy 2: betas from about 13 September 2019, feature-complete declared at beta 18, RC1 on 3 April 2020 ("production-ready ... reserving another cycle for last-minute bug fixes", "deploy it into production, and let us know how it goes"), at least three RCs, GA 4 May 2020 (pre-2024). — [Caddy announcements index](https://caddy.community/c/announcements/8?page=2); [Caddy 2 beta 18](https://caddy.community/t/caddy-2-beta-18-now-available/7256); [Caddy 2 RC1](https://caddy.community/t/caddy-2-release-candidate-1/7415); [Caddy v2.0.0](https://github.com/caddyserver/caddy/releases/tag/v2.0.0)
- Traefik 3: "at least 2 betas" to give "ample time for the community to explore and stress-test"; December 2022 beta → 14 February 2024 RC → 29 April 2024 GA. — [Traefik 3.0 beta programme](https://traefik.io/blog/traefik-proxy-3-0-scope-beta-program-and-the-first-feature-drop); [Traefik release table](https://doc.traefik.io/traefik/v3.0/deprecation/releases/)
- HAProxy: an LTS point-zero has had about six months of stabilisation; first-time users are told to use their operating system's version; odd branches are for "developers and highly skilled users". — [HAProxy BRANCHES file](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/haproxy/haproxy/master/BRANCHES)
- Valkey 8.0: about six weeks from rc1 (1 August 2024) to GA (15–16 September 2024) with at least two RCs, on a mature inherited codebase and after a compatibility-only first release. — [Valkey 8.0.0-rc1](https://github.com/valkey-io/valkey/releases/tag/8.0.0-rc1); [Valkey 8 GA post](https://valkey.io/blog/valkey-8-ga/)
- Pingora: years of production at its origin company before a public 0.1, and still 0.x after more than two further years. — [Cloudflare blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/pingora-open-source); [Phoronix on 0.1](https://Phoronix.com/news/Cloudflare-Pingora-0.1)
- WireGuard: public development from 2015, 1.0.0 in March 2020, with an external audit reported before the label (pre-2024). — [WireGuard 1.0.0 announcement](https://lists.zx2c4.com/pipermail/wireguard/2020-March/005206.html); [TechNadu coverage](https://www.technadu.com/wireguard-vpn-reaches-version-1-0-0-and-gets-into-linux-kernel-5-6/97333/)
- curl's bounty, over its life: 87 confirmed vulnerabilities, more than USD 100,000 paid (funded "for many years" by the Internet Bug Bounty); the confirmed rate fell from above 15 % to below 5 % from 2025 ("Not even one in twenty was real"); the programme ended 31 January 2026; no reward of any size is now paid and no reporter fee was introduced. — [The end of the curl bug bounty](https://daniel.haxx.se/blog/2026/01/26/the-end-of-the-curl-bug-bounty/)
- HackerOne's Internet Bug Bounty: "Effective March 27, the IBB program has been paused for new submissions"; reporting attributes it to an imbalance between AI-accelerated discovery and maintainers' capacity to fix; Node.js paused its own bounty citing loss of that funding; the programme was still listed as paused on 6 October 2026 (the last point from one secondary report). — [HackerOne IBB policy versions](https://hackerone.com/ibb/policy_versions); [Dark Reading](https://www.darkreading.com/application-security/ai-led-remediation-crisis-prompts-hackerone-pause-bug-bounties%250A); [Slashdot/InfoWorld](https://tech.slashdot.org/story/26/04/06/0113254/); [Privacy Guides](https://www.privacyguides.org/news/2026/04/17/hackerone-pauses-internet-bug-bounty/)
- huntr is "sunsetting the traditional OSS vulnerability program" in favour of AI-security challenges (launch 12 June 2026). — [Huntr 2.0 FAQ](https://blog.huntr.com/huntr-2-0-faq)
- Claims that curl accepted no vulnerability reports at all from 1 July to 3 August 2026 appear only on secondary blogs and were not confirmed from a curl source. — [Pinggy blog](https://pinggy.io/blog/curl_ai_slop_summer_of_bliss/) (unverified)
- What replaced bounties in practice for curl: free private reporting, funded audits, and AI-assisted scanning partners whose results are listed publicly. — [curl security audits page](https://curl.se/docs/audits.html)

### Inferences
- No project publishes a number of deployments required for "stable"; the observable minimums are *time with a frozen feature set in other people's production* (HAProxy: about 6 months; Caddy 2: 1 month of RCs after about 7 months of betas; Traefik 3: 2.5 months of RC after 14 months of betas) and *an explicit request to run RCs in production*. For a server whose protocol stacks are all its own and whose user count is a handful, the HAProxy figure (about six months between feature freeze and the stable label) is the defensible floor, with the protocol code unchanged except for fixes during it.
- With bounties gone, the substitutes for "outsiders have attacked this" are: an audit report, the public interop matrix, a public test instance that invites attack with the attack-suite rows documented, AI-assisted scanning runs with findings listed (the curl/Aisle pattern), and distribution packagers' own review. A young project should not open a paid bounty in 2026: the experience of curl is that it buys triage load, not findings.
- Canary users matter more than count: a few named operators running different workloads (static hosting, PHP hosting with many sites, reverse proxy with WebSockets, HTTP/3 to real mobile clients on lossy paths) for the whole RC period, each with a way to report and an agreed rollback, cover more of the state space than hundreds of identical idle installs. The project's own history (the blocking-socket freeze found by a phone on a TLS WebSocket; QUIC bugs found by the interop runner in a day) is the argument.

**Suggested staging (synthesis of the six sections; an inference, not a cited standard)**

*Alpha → Beta (entry criteria: the product stops changing shape)*
1. Feature scope for 1.0 written down, including what is explicitly *not* in it and which features are "experimental, off by default, outside the security-support promise" (freenginx lesson).
2. Configuration format, CLI, control-plane API and log formats frozen: from here changes need a deprecation warning and a migration path (Caddy 1→2 lesson). Migration mechanism exists.
3. SECURITY.md with private reporting, 14-day response commitment, supported-versions table; GitHub advisories enabled as the CVE route (Best Practices passing; Scorecard Security-Policy).
4. Hardened build flags reconciled with the OpenSSF guide of August 2026, each measured; sanitizer and fuzz jobs continuous (ClusterFuzzLite), corpora kept, coverage numbers published.
5. Decoded-size limits for HPACK/QPACK verified against the header-accordion pattern; resource-exhaustion defaults reviewed for every protocol.
6. OpenSSF Best Practices "passing" badge obtained; Scorecard run and published with notes on the solo-maintainer checks.
7. Signed release artefacts and repository metadata, SBOM per release, provenance from the release job.

*Beta (several months; at least two betas, per Traefik) → RC (entry: outside verification has happened)*
8. Server image submitted to the public QUIC interop matrix; h2spec/HTTP/3 error-suite results and HTTP Garden-style differential results published per release.
9. Independent audit of the pre-authentication network surface and the privileged helper commissioned (25+ person-days; funder sought through OSTIF/Sovereign Tech/Alpha-Omega), findings fixed, fix review done, report published (Kea/Stork and ngtcp2 sequence).
10. Soak and fault-injection runs defined and automated; results (memory, descriptors, errors over N days) attached to each beta.
11. Named canary operators on distinct workloads; a public test instance.
12. Branch and maintenance policy written (BRANCHES/RELEASES model): one stable branch, its window, backport class, release and security-release procedure, embargo handling for packagers.
13. Operations guide, upgrade/rollback guide, migration guides from nginx and Apache, troubleshooting; systemd unit shipped with a published `systemd-analyze security` score.
14. Statement on AI-assisted development and review responsibility; statement on continuity (second key holder, reproducible release job).

*RC (feature-frozen, "production-ready, reserving a cycle for last-minute fixes", per Caddy) → 1.0 (entry: time has passed without surprises)*
15. About six months since the feature freeze with protocol code changed only by fixes (HAProxy's stabilisation interval), of which at least one full RC cycle of four to ten weeks with RCs deployed in the canaries' production.
16. No open medium-or-higher security finding; every audit finding closed or publicly accepted with a reason.
17. One real upgrade exercised across the frozen config (beta → RC → 1.0) on the canaries without manual edits; rollback exercised.
18. The 1.0 announcement states the definition of stable ("stable, not perfect"), the support window and end date, the compatibility promise for 1.x, the supported platforms, and what is experimental.

### Gaps
- No source gives deployment counts at the time of any comparable project's stable label; the staging above rests on durations and stated practices only.
- Results of bug bounties specifically for *young* projects (as opposed to curl and Node.js) were not found.
- No source was found on how public "attack me" test instances were run by comparable servers or what they yielded.
- Google's open-source vulnerability reward programme changes in March 2026 (reported as ceasing to accept AI-generated reports, and elsewhere as a partial suspension) were seen only in secondary coverage and not verified.
- Search-result pages and fetched pages contained no embedded instructions directed at the reader or an agent; nothing was acted on beyond reading.
